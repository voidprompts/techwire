CoreWeave’s first quarterly filing after its 2025 IPO illustrates a central tension in AI infrastructure: demand can grow rapidly while the provider must finance expensive capacity before all of its commercial assumptions are proven. The filing gives readers evidence for assessing that trade-off without treating growth as automatically profitable or debt as automatically fatal.

## What the public record says

CoreWeave’s [quarterly report hosted by the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1769628/000176962825000014/crwv-20250331.htm) says its March 2025 IPO issued 36.59 million Class A shares at $40 each and generated approximately $1.4 billion in net proceeds after underwriting costs and other expenses. The report also details financing arrangements, infrastructure commitments and customer concentration.

These figures come from the company’s filing. They describe a reporting period, not the company’s current condition, and should be read alongside later filings before making financial decisions.

## Capacity changes the startup playbook

A software startup can often add users without buying dedicated physical equipment for each contract. An AI cloud operator faces a different sequence: it must secure chips, data-center space, power and networking, then keep that capacity productively occupied. Financing and supply agreements become part of the product strategy.

Customer concentration can accelerate early scale because a few large contracts support major infrastructure commitments. It also creates dependency: changes in one customer’s demand, credit or deployment schedule can have an outsized effect. Contracted revenue should therefore be considered alongside cancellation terms, delivery obligations and the cost of capacity.

## Metrics worth watching

A disciplined review of an infrastructure business asks:

* how much revenue depends on the largest customers;
* whether capital spending and financing costs rise faster than cash generation;
* how long equipment remains competitive;
* whether contracted capacity is available on schedule;
* and how debt covenants or collateral affect flexibility.

None of those questions can be answered by an IPO valuation alone. They require the notes and risk disclosures in successive reports.
