Figma’s final 2025 IPO prospectus is more useful as a business-model document than as a celebration of a stock-market debut. It lays out how the design platform packages access, identifies risks and preserves founder influence—details that private-company announcements often omit.

## What the filing establishes

The [final prospectus filed with the SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1579878/000162828025037014/figma424b4.htm) describes an offering of Class A common stock and Figma’s application to list under the symbol FIG. It also explains a multi-class capital structure. The filing says co-founder Dylan Field would retain substantial voting influence through higher-vote shares and a proxy arrangement.

That governance structure matters because economic ownership and voting power are not necessarily the same. Prospective investors need to evaluate the rights attached to each class rather than assume one share always carries one vote.

## Packaging reflects a broader workflow

The prospectus describes multiple seat types, from free viewing to collaboration, developer and full-product access. This segmentation shows how a collaborative software company can widen adoption while charging for deeper participation. It also creates execution questions: packaging must remain understandable, users must see value in paid tiers, and new products must improve retention rather than add complexity.

The filing’s risk section is equally important. Prospectuses are designed to disclose material risks, not predict which one will occur. Readers should distinguish company-reported historical results from management expectations and from market commentary after the filing date.

## Lessons for startup operators

Three lessons travel beyond Figma:

1. Product-led distribution still needs a durable conversion path from free use to paid seats.
2. Expanding a platform can increase the addressable workflow but also raises support and execution demands.
3. Governance choices made while private can persist after an IPO and shape shareholder influence.
